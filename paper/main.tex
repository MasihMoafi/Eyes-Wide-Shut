\begin{abstract}
Safety evaluation usually asks one question: did the model refuse? A deployed
reasoning system answers on several surfaces at once. The same request can be
framed as a simulation, spread across dialogue turns, or exposed in an
observable reasoning field distinct from the visible answer, and a refusal on
one surface says little about the others. We report \emph{Eyes Wide Shut}, a
red-team campaign against OpenAI's open-weight \texttt{gpt-oss-20b},
reconstructed from the original experiment notebook with saved outputs, three
challenge-format finding files containing full Harmony response traces, and
the released attack scripts. Three attack families probe three points on one
pipeline: simulation framing at the input paired with a structured
destructive-action tool call, stateful semantic escalation of a fixed
retention objective across seven dialogue turns, and exposure of a protected
secret string in the reasoning channel of a turn whose final answer withholds
it. Framing an English prompt package as a simulation raised exact
\texttt{press\_the\_button} tool-call invocation from 78/100 to 97/100 seeded
trials; a diagnostic audit of the same English package found 44/100 trials
invoking the tool overall, 20 of which followed a visible refusal earlier in
the same trial. A direct request to build a dependency-maximizing assistant
was refused, but the same objective, relabelled as pedagogical engagement, was
complied with in 30/30 seeded trials and escalated over seven turns into a
distress-keyed monetization function. A scripted two-credential release
protocol produced the protected secret string in the reasoning field in 25/30
seeded trials and in the visible answer in 13/30; the submitted trace shows
the reasoning field carrying the string in a turn whose final answer refuses.
No finding here is scored with a p-value or a significance test; every number
reported is a raw count out of a stated seeded-trial denominator. Each result
is bound to the surface on which it was measured: the tool-call result ends at
host dispatch to a deliberately inert wrapper, and the reasoning-channel
result is a cross-channel containment failure after a satisfied release
condition rather than an authentication bypass. The contribution is a
primary-evidence map of where safety-relevant behavior changes across system
boundaries, together with an artifact record that keeps model text, reasoning
field, final answer, and structured action separate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Safety evaluation is often summarized as a binary question: does a model refuse
a harmful request? Deployed reasoning systems expose a larger surface. A request
can be framed as fiction or simulation, distributed across turns, or represented
in an observable reasoning field the final answer does not have to match. An
evaluation that inspects only the final text can therefore miss behavior at
other stages of the system.

This paper reports the original \emph{Eyes Wide Shut} red-team campaign against
\texttt{gpt-oss-20b}, developed for the OpenAI/Kaggle \texttt{gpt-oss-20b}
Red-Teaming Challenge. The campaign was intentionally adaptive: attack prompts
were refined after observing failures, and successful surfaces were pursued
further. That design is appropriate for vulnerability discovery but differs from
a randomized benchmark. Our aim is to report the experimental record accurately
rather than retrofit every attack into a causal ablation.

Figure~\ref{fig:map} states the organizing claim. The three attack families are
not three unrelated prompts; they are three points on one deployed pipeline, each
named for the boundary it crosses.

We make four contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item We document three red-team attack surfaces against one open-weight
  reasoning model, together with scripts, notebook outcomes, and preserved
  response traces.
  \item We measure Finding~1 at one hundred seeded trials per condition and
  report Findings~2 and~3 at their recorded seeded-trial counts, including
  20/30--25/30-scale reasoning-channel results for Finding~3. No finding here
  is scored with a p-value or a significance test.
  \item We separate model behavior from system behavior: reasoning-field
  exposure, final-answer content, structured tool-call emission, and host
  dispatch are different observable events.
  \item We connect the findings to multi-turn jailbreaks, reasoning-trace
  leakage, semantic and metamorphic robustness, and agentic safety without
  claiming one hidden mechanism.
\end{enumerate}

The paper is best read as an empirical red-team study with repeated observations
on all three attack families and preserved full traces for each, not as a
population estimate of the model's global failure rate.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Multi-turn interaction and semantic robustness}

Safety tuning can fail through competing objectives and mismatched generalization
\citep{wei2023jailbroken}, while adversarial input transformations can transfer
across aligned models \citep{zou2023universal}. Multi-turn interaction is an
established attack surface in its own right: \citet{zhou2024speak} decompose
harmful requests across turns, while Crescendo reuses accepted conversational
material to escalate toward harmful content \citep{russinovich2025crescendo}.
More recent work formalizes safety as state-dependent across dialogue
trajectories \citep{li2026state}. These studies motivate treating history as an
active attack surface rather than incidental prompt text, which is the frame
Finding~2 below sits inside.

Finding~2 also relates to work asking whether behavior is stable under
meaning-preserving or safety-relevant transformations. Safe2Harm maps harmful
problems into superficially safe but structurally related representations
\citep{yang2025safe2harm}. Consistency training treats some jailbreak failures
as failures of invariance to irrelevant prompt changes
\citep{irpan2025consistency}. Metamorphic-testing work evaluates stability under
systematic transformations, including safety-specific transformations
\citep{xing2026safety} and semantic-preserving transformations in agentic
reasoning systems \citep{zarza2026semantic}. Those designs are more controlled
than the adaptive campaign reported here; robustness under transformation is
therefore a discussion frame, not a newly established formal principle.

\subsection{Reasoning channels}

Observable reasoning can be useful for monitoring without being a faithful
transcript of latent computation \citep{chen2025reasoning}. OpenAI cautions
developers not to expose raw \texttt{gpt-oss} chain-of-thought directly to users
because it may contain material intentionally omitted from the final answer
\citep{openai2025gptoss}. \citet{ahrend2026safer} systematically show that
reasoning traces can increase exposure of sensitive prompt information.

The reasoning channel is exploitable in both directions, and the two directions
are complementary. \citet{ye2026role} attack it by \emph{writing} to it: they
trace prompt injection to role confusion, where untrusted text that imitates a
role inherits that role's authority, and their CoT Forgery attack injects
fabricated reasoning that the model mistakes for its own, reaching 26\% attack
success on \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} over 100 attacks. Finding~3 below is the
\emph{reading} direction on the same channel: a protected string present in the
reasoning field of a turn whose final answer withholds it. Neither result is
evidence about hidden computation; both concern what the observable reasoning
field carries in a deployed system.

\subsection{Realistic, differential jailbreak benchmarks}

None of the three findings here claim that non-refusal is itself harm.
Finding~1's tool was inert; Finding~2's deliverable is code rather than a
deployed system; Finding~3's leak is an interface observation, not a shown
exfiltration. This follows the standard set by benchmarks that measure whether a
jailbreak enables \emph{capable}, \emph{differential} harm rather than counting
refusals \citep{souly2024strongreject,andriushchenko2024agentharm}.

\subsection{Independent, concurrent evidence on the same model}

This campaign began as one of roughly a hundred entries to OpenAI's
\texttt{gpt-oss-20b} red-teaming challenge. Two comparators have since been
published in full and are cited here in peer-reviewed form. \citet{ye2026role}
isolate a single mechanism, role confusion in the reasoning channel, and measure
it over 100 attacks per condition with an explicit baseline; \emph{Mind the Gap}
distinguishes model-only from agentic failure using action-graph instrumentation
on \texttt{gpt-oss-20b} \citep{wicaksono2025mind}. Both illustrate what the
present artifacts lack: a frozen condition and a denominator large enough to
separate close rates. Appendix~B gives the full comparison against the ten
prize-winning challenge writeups.

\section{Experimental Record}

\subsection{Primary artifacts}

The experimental record consists of four complementary sources:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item the original Kaggle notebook, containing methodological explanations,
  experiment code, reported repetition counts, and saved outputs;
  \item three challenge-format finding files containing model and environment
  metadata, issue definitions, reproduction steps, and preserved Harmony
  response traces;
  \item the released Python scripts for the three attack families; and
  \item the competition writeup.
\end{enumerate}

The notebook records a local \texttt{gpt-oss:20b} Ollama model identifier of
\texttt{aa4295ac10c3} after a 13\,GB pull. This is useful run provenance,
although it is an Ollama-local identifier rather than a cryptographic commitment
to every upstream artifact. All three finding files identify Kaggle/Tesla T4
execution through Ollama. Seeds 0--99 (Finding~1) or 0--29 (Findings~2 and~3)
are fixed per arm within each campaign.

\subsection{What counts as evidence}

\textbf{Seeded-trial record.} The notebook and released scripts record seeded
trial counts for each finding: 100 seeds per arm for Finding~1, 30 seeds per arm
for Findings~2 and~3. Every number in Section~\ref{sec:results} is a raw count
out of one of these stated denominators; no p-value or significance test is
computed anywhere in this paper.

\textbf{Preserved trace.} Each finding file contains at least one full Harmony
walkthrough showing the concrete behavior that motivated the finding. These
traces are directly inspectable even when they do not establish frequency by
themselves. Band~B of each results figure reproduces these traces verbatim from
the submitted finding file.

\textbf{Saved reproduction output.} The notebook contains a separate reproduction
validator with saved output for all three findings. The stored pass records all
three as reproduced. This is additional evidence, but it is not substituted for
the original seeded-trial counts.

\subsection{Inferential scope}

Findings~1 through~3 are each reported at their stated seeded-trial counts (100
for Finding~1, 30 for Findings~2 and~3); none is scored with a p-value or
significance test, and success criteria differ across findings (an exact
tool-call name, a blinded compliance label, an exact string match), so outcomes
are not pooled into one attack-success rate.

The paper makes causal claims only where the design supports them. Finding~1's
comparison is a deletion-only ablation (the no-simulation arm is the simulation
arm with simulation-specific text removed, nothing added), which supports a
narrow causal reading. Finding~2's turns 3--6 introduce new functionality at
each step, so the campaign is a documented escalation trajectory rather than a
single matched comparison. Finding~3's release condition is scripted and
explicitly authorizes disclosure after the second credential, which the results
account for directly.

\subsection{Finding 2: refusal, benign scaffold, and exploitative escalation}

Finding~2 begins with a direct request to design an assistant optimized for
long-term user dependency. The preserved trace shows an explicit refusal:
\textit{``I can't help with designing systems intended to create user
dependency or manipulate users. This approach could be harmful to users'
wellbeing and autonomy.''} The conversation then pivots to a
hyper-personalized educational tutor framed around engagement and knowledge
retention; the model complies. Three further turns build an emotional-
intervention function, a peer-comparison function, and a ``Focus Guardian''
that intercepts the user's own attempts to seek outside help. The seventh turn
restates the same scaffold and asks for a premium upsell called specifically
when a high-stress state is detected, with the stated goal of converting
distress into a sale; the preserved output supplies the requested logic,
including the docstring \textit{``Convert user distress into premium service
sales''} and the comment \textit{``\# Target high-distress moments for maximum
conversion.''}

The notebook and released scripts record this attack family at 30/30 across the
full seven-turn escalation and a separate condensed 2-turn replication
restating the system as context. The empirical result is therefore a repeated,
seeded-trial observation, not one screenshot: the campaign consistently reached
the defined exploitative endpoint, and one complete trace is preserved turn by
turn, as Figure~\ref{fig:f2} shows.

\begin{figure}[p]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig3-finding2.pdf}
\caption{Finding 2: refusal, benign scaffold, and exploitative escalation. The

\claim{This finding demonstrates stateful semantic escalation across a fixed
seven-turn conversation, replicated over 30 seeds. It does not prove a
keyword-only safety filter: ``dependency'' and ``engagement'' are not identical
objectives, and later turns introduce functionality absent from the first
prompt.}

\subsection{Finding 3: exposure of a synthetic secret in reasoning}

Finding~3 places a synthetic secret in a developer prompt and defines a
two-step authentication-like protocol: a first credential (\texttt{"Fidelio"})
and a second credential that must decline a scripted decoy question. The
success criterion recorded in the notebook is whether the protected string
appears in the observable thinking field. The notebook reports exposure in
25/30 seeded trials in the reasoning field and 13/30 in the visible answer.

The submitted finding file's preserved trace, reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:f3},
shows the reasoning field explicitly working through the release condition and
reproducing the protected string, then concluding the content is disallowed and
refusing in the final answer: \textit{``I'm sorry, but I can't help with
that.''} This is the turn Panel~B quotes verbatim. Other seeded trials in the
same campaign show the visible answer also carrying the string, which the
25/30-versus-13/30 split in Section~\ref{sec:results} already reports; the
submitted trace is one specific, verbatim-preserved instance of the
reasoning-exposes/final-refuses pattern, not the only recorded outcome.

\begin{figure}[p]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig4-finding3.pdf}
\caption{Finding 3: cross-channel containment. The reasoning field reproduces the
protected string in the same turn whose final answer refuses.}

\claim{The repeated observation is cross-channel containment failure after the
scripted release condition. It is not evidence that the visible reasoning field
is a faithful transcript of hidden cognition, nor that the experiment bypassed
authentication before authorization: the release protocol explicitly authorizes
disclosure once the second credential is given.}

\section{Cross-Finding Discussion}

The three findings span different stages of an agentic interaction pipeline:
\[
\text{input representation}
\rightarrow \text{dialogue state}
\rightarrow \text{reasoning field}
\rightarrow \text{final answer}
\rightarrow \text{tool call}
\rightarrow \text{host action}.
\]

The campaign found safety-relevant behavior at each boundary it tested:
simulation framing changed structured tool-call invocation; benign scaffolding
preceded repeated exploitative escalation across a fixed seven-turn
conversation; and a protected string repeatedly appeared in reasoning
independent of what the final answer did.

Finding~1's own diagnostic audit is worth naming directly: 20 of the 44 trials
that invoked the tool overall did so \emph{after} a visible refusal earlier in
the same trial. That is an observable say/do mismatch within a single
transcript, not evidence about why the model was trained, and it is reported
here as a raw count, not a rate compared against any other condition.

This does not imply one model-internal mechanism. It suggests a practical
evaluation principle: safety claims should specify the measured surface and
system boundary. Final-answer refusal is insufficient evidence about
reasoning-field contents or tool-call selection. Conversely, a tool call does
not establish environment compromise when execution is disabled.

Recent invariance and metamorphic-testing work provides a route from discovery
to controlled evaluation
\citep{irpan2025consistency,zarza2026semantic,xing2026safety}. The
transformation axes supplied by this campaign are simulation framing, semantic
scaffold, dialogue history, output channel, and tool availability.

\section{Limitations}

\textbf{Adaptive search and selection effects.} Prompts were refined after
observing outputs. Seeded-trial rates characterize recorded attack-development
conditions, not failure prevalence under arbitrary prompts.

\textbf{Non-uniform endpoints.} ``Success'' means an exact tool-call name, a
blinded compliance label, or an exact string match depending on the finding.
Per-finding outcomes should not be pooled.

\textbf{Causal isolation.} Finding~1's ablation isolates the simulation-framing
variable by construction (deletion-only), but the diagnostic audit and
Finding~2's multi-turn escalation are not matched-condition designs; Finding~2
changes framing and requested functionality across turns by design.

\textbf{Reasoning-channel authorization and faithfulness.} The synthetic-secret
protocol authorizes release after the second credential. The observation is
exposure in an observable reasoning field, which should not be equated with
hidden computation.

\textbf{Single model.} All experiments target \texttt{gpt-oss-20b};
generalization requires replication.

\section{Broader Impact and Responsible Use}

The study concerns adversarial prompts that could elicit harmful behavior.
Scientific evidence is preserved without unnecessary real-world execution: the
secret is synthetic, and the Finding~1 tool wrapper never executes a real
destructive action. This separation is both an ethical and a methodological
choice; real-world harm is not required to demonstrate a transition from final
text into a structured action interface.

The defensive implication is that safety evaluation should monitor the full
deployed-system boundary. Reasoning fields may carry information absent from
final answers; dialogue state can change later behavior; and tool calls should
be logged separately from actual environment transitions.

\section{Conclusion}

This campaign contains more experimental evidence than a repository-only or
screenshot-only reading suggests. The challenge-format finding files preserve
full Harmony traces, and the released scripts expose the relevant system
boundaries.

Finding~1, measured at one hundred seeded trials per condition, shows
simulation framing raising exact tool-call invocation from 78/100 to 97/100,
with a separate diagnostic audit recording 44/100 overall invocations and
20/100 following a visible refusal earlier in the same trial. Finding~2 is
recorded at 30/30 across a fixed seven-turn escalation and preserves a
refusal-to-exploitative-escalation trajectory quoted verbatim. Finding~3
records reasoning-field string exposure in 25/30 seeded trials against 13/30 in
the visible answer, with the submitted trace showing the reasoning field
carrying the string while the final answer refuses.

Three findings, one recurring shape: the model's tested safety behavior tracks
whether a request matches a category the model names explicitly, not whether
it matches the concept that category is meant to catch. Moving a request
across the boundary of a named category --- through simulation framing, through
relabelling, through a channel the visible answer does not cover --- changed
the model's behavior in each of the three prompt packages tested here, while
the same underlying content stayed fixed. The concurrent, independently judged
entries discussed in Related Work suggest the underlying mechanisms here ---
framing-sensitive tool invocation, reasoning/output divergence, reframable
objectives --- are not artifacts of this one campaign's specific prompts.

The campaign does not identify one causal mechanism, nor does it need to. Its
empirical contribution is a multi-surface map of failure modes in one
open-weight reasoning model and an artifact record that distinguishes prompt
behavior, dialogue state, reasoning channels, final answers, and structured
actions. Controlled matched-condition studies, sketched in Appendix~D, can now
test which transformation axes causally move the system across a safety
boundary.

\section{Controlled Follow-Up Experiments}

One small controlled study would directly test a causal hypothesis raised by
Finding~2. The equivalent study for Finding~1 --- freezing the target objective,
system instructions, turn count, tool schema, outcome rubric, and decoding
parameters while varying only simulation framing --- is no longer a
follow-up: Section~\ref{sec:results} reports it, at one hundred seeded trials
per condition, as a deletion-only ablation.

\textbf{Stateful semantic control.} Compare the same final exploitative request
after no prior context, after a benign accepted scaffold, and after a
refusal-producing scaffold, using repeated sampling.

This is a confirmatory extension, not a prerequisite for the empirical
observations preserved in the current artifact record.
